10 Study Techniques Every Student Should Know

Written by Sarah Jenkins, Academic Skills Fellow

For generations, the default student study routine has remained virtually unchanged: open a massive textbook, grab a bright yellow highlighter, and read through hundreds of pages while hoping the concepts absorb by osmosis. Yet decades of experimental cognitive psychology demonstrate that highlighting, rereading, and summarizing are among the least effective learning strategies available. They create what psychologists call the fluency illusion—the deceptive feeling that because text feels easy to read, it has been deeply encoded into long-term memory.

When exam day arrives and you are faced with a blank answer sheet, recognition is useless. What you need is retrieval capability. Below are ten rigorous, evidence-backed study techniques designed to restructure how you encode, organize, and recall complex academic material.

1. Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)

Active recall requires you to retrieve information from memory without looking at your notes. Instead of passively reading that the French Revolution began in 1789, you generate a prompt: “What historical conditions triggered the events of 1789 in France?” and answer it from memory.

Practical Example: When reading a textbook, cover the paragraph you just finished with an index card. Force yourself to articulate its core thesis out loud or write down the key formula. Every time you struggle to retrieve a concept, your brain strengthens the synaptic pathways associated with that memory trace.

2. Spaced Repetition (The Leitner System)

In 1885, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus discovered the “forgetting curve”: humans lose approximately 70% of newly learned information within 24 hours unless it is reviewed. Spaced repetition counteracts this by reviewing material at gradually increasing intervals (e.g., Day 1, Day 3, Day 7, Day 14, Day 30).

Practical Example: Implement the Leitner Box System. Label five flashcard boxes 1 through 5. Cards in Box 1 are reviewed daily; cards in Box 2 every three days; Box 3 weekly. When you answer a card correctly, advance it to the next box. If you answer incorrectly, return it immediately to Box 1. This guarantees you spend time on your weakest concepts.

3. The Feynman Technique (Teach to Learn)

Named after Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman, this method posits that you truly understand a concept only if you can explain it in simple, jargon-free language to a beginner.

💡 The 4-Step Feynman Algorithm

  1. Write the concept name at the top of a blank sheet of paper (e.g., “Photosynthesis” or “Bayesian Probability”).
  2. Explain the concept out loud or in writing as if teaching an inquisitive middle-school student. Use analogies and everyday words.
  3. When you hit a stumbling block or resort to dense technical jargon, stop. That friction is your precise knowledge gap.
  4. Return to your textbook or lecture recordings to clarify that specific sub-point, then simplify your explanation again.

4. The Pomodoro Focus Protocol

Attention is a finite physiological resource. Cognitive fatigue sets in after extended periods of continuous intellectual exertion. The Pomodoro technique divides study sessions into focused 25-minute sprints separated by mandatory 5-minute cognitive breaks.

Practical Example: During the 25-minute sprint, close all unrelated browser tabs, put your phone in another room, and work single-mindedly on one task. During the 5-minute break, step away from screens entirely: drink water, stretch, or look out a window. After four cycles, enjoy a longer 25-minute restorative rest.

5. The Cornell Note-Taking System

Developed by Walter Pauk at Cornell University, this formatting framework turns raw lecture notes into self-contained study guides. Divide your page into three distinct sections:

  • The Notes Column (Right 70%): Record core ideas, formulas, and diagrams during class using bullet points and abbreviations.
  • The Cue Column (Left 30%): Immediately after class, write guiding questions, keywords, and vocabulary prompts corresponding to the notes.
  • The Summary Section (Bottom 2 inches): In two or three sentences, synthesize the big-picture takeaway of the entire page in your own words.

To study, simply cover the right column and use the left cue column as an instant self-quiz.

6. Dual Coding (Visual & Verbal Synthesis)

Dual coding theory suggests that the human brain processes visual and verbal information through separate cognitive channels. When you pair written notes with spatial diagrams, concept maps, or timelines, you create two distinct memory traces instead of one.

Practical Example: When studying cellular respiration in biology, do not just memorize written bullet points of the Krebs Cycle. Sketch a circular flowchart with arrows indicating where ATP and NADH are generated. The combination of visual geometry and verbal logic makes recall during exams significantly easier.

7. Blurting (Brain-Dumping)

Blurting is a high-intensity active recall technique especially potent for exam revision. Select a specific topic from your module syllabus, set a timer for ten minutes, and write down every formula, definition, historical date, and connection you can summon from memory on a blank whiteboard or paper.

Practical Example: Once the timer expires, compare your brain dump against your master lecture slides. Use a red pen to write in everything you omitted or misstated. The items written in red immediately become your flashcard queue for tomorrow morning.

8. Interleaving vs. Blocked Practice

Most students practice blocked study: solving 30 identical quadratic equation problems, followed by 30 geometry problems. While blocked practice feels comfortable, it fails to teach an essential exam skill: problem classification.

Practical Example: Practice interleaving by shuffling problem sets from different chapters. Mix calculus integration, matrix algebra, and vector problems into one homework session. This forces your brain to first diagnose which rule applies before executing the mathematical computation.

9. The PQ4R Method

Designed specifically for mastering dense academic literature and scientific papers, the PQ4R acronym provides a systematic sequence:

  • Preview: Scan chapter titles, section headings, graphs, and introduction summaries to build a mental scaffold.
  • Question: Turn headings into questions before reading (e.g., “What are the ecological effects of ocean acidification?”).
  • Read: Read actively with the specific goal of answering your formulated questions.
  • Reflect: Pause and connect the new information to what you learned in previous lectures.
  • Recite: Speak or write the answers to your questions from memory without consulting the page.
  • Review: Revisit the chapter outline at the end of the week to test your retention.

10. Environmental Priming & State-Dependent Recall

Your physical environment significantly influences mental focus. If you study in bed, your brain associates that posture with sleep and relaxation, leading to cognitive lethargy. Conversely, establishing a dedicated, uncluttered desk space reserved exclusively for academic work primes your nervous system for high focus.

Practical Example: Match study conditions to exam conditions. If your final exam takes place in a quiet, cool room at 9:00 AM, conduct your timed practice papers in a quiet library room at 9:00 AM. Even subtle environmental cues like room temperature and seated posture improve retrieval fluency on test day.

“You do not rise to the level of your exam ambitions. You fall to the level of your study systems.” — The Student Corner Learning Principles

Conclusion: Building Your Personal System

You do not need to implement all ten techniques tomorrow morning. Pick two methods that address your greatest current academic challenge—such as active recall and the Cornell system if you struggle with lecture notes, or interleaving and blurting if preparing for STEM exams. Practice them consistently for three weeks and watch your retention, speed, and confidence transform.
